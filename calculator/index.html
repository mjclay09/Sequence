How it works

The math, step by step

  1. Find the concentration. Divide the peptide in the vial by the water you add. 10 mg ÷ 2 mL = 5 mg per mL
  2. Match the units. Convert your dose to mg. 500 mcg = 0.5 mg
  3. Find the volume. Divide your dose by the concentration. 0.5 mg ÷ 5 mg/mL = 0.1 mL
  4. Convert to units. On a U-100 syringe, multiply mL by 100. 0.1 mL × 100 = 10 units

Tips for measuring accurately

  • Pick a water amount that puts your dose on a whole-number line, and between about 10 and 50 units on a 100-unit syringe. Very small draws are hard to measure.
  • More water does not weaken the vial. It spreads the same amount over more liquid, so you draw more units.
  • Write the concentration and the date on the vial after mixing, so you never have to redo the math.
  • Follow your pharmacy’s instructions for mixing, storage and how long the vial lasts once mixed.
  • Double-check your numbers with your pharmacist the first time.

Common questions

How much bacteriostatic water should I add to my peptide vial?

Any amount works. The water only changes how concentrated the liquid is, not how much peptide is in the vial. Pick an amount that makes your prescribed dose easy to measure, ideally a whole number of units between about 10 and 50 on a 100-unit syringe. The calculator shows the easiest option for your numbers.

What does 1 unit mean on an insulin syringe?

On a standard U-100 insulin syringe, 100 units equals 1 mL, so 1 unit is 0.01 mL. A 30-unit syringe holds 0.3 mL and a 50-unit syringe holds 0.5 mL.

How do I convert mcg to units?

First work out the concentration: milligrams in the vial divided by milliliters of water. Convert your dose to milligrams (1,000 mcg = 1 mg), divide it by the concentration to get milliliters, then multiply by 100 to get units on a U-100 syringe.

How many doses are in my vial?

Divide the amount in the vial by your dose, using the same unit for both. A 10 mg vial holds 20 doses of 500 mcg. In practice you may get slightly fewer because a little liquid stays in the vial.

Does adding more water make a peptide weaker?

Each unit on the syringe holds less peptide, but the total in the vial stays the same. You just draw more units to get the same dose.
